01 · The Problem

Find the lowest point of a landscape you cannot see

Optimization means finding the input x that makes some function f(x) as small as possible — a cost, an error, a loss, a distance. Picture f as a landscape: the height at each point is how bad that solution is, and we want the deepest valley.

The catch: in real problems the landscape is invisible. We can only probe one point at a time and get back a single number. Try it below.

—

Local minimum

A point lower than everything immediately around it — but not the lowest overall. A small dip on a mountainside. Simple downhill methods get trapped here.

Global minimum

The lowest point of the entire landscape — the answer we actually want. On a "multimodal" landscape there are many local minima hiding it.

Why a swarm?

Many searchers probing different places at once — and sharing what they find — are far less likely to get stuck in a dip than a single searcher. That is the core idea of PSO.

02 · The Intuition

A flock of birds searching for food

In 1995, social psychologist James Kennedy and electrical engineer Russell Eberhart were simulating how bird flocks move. They noticed their simulated flock was surprisingly good at finding things. Particle Swarm Optimization is that observation turned into an algorithm.

No bird knows where the food is. No bird can see the whole field. Each bird only knows how strong the smell is right where it is. Yet the flock finds the food — by every bird following three simple rules:

Keep flying the way you're going

Birds have momentum. They don't stop and turn instantly; they carry on roughly in their current direction.

Remember your own best spot

Each bird remembers where it smelled the food most strongly, and feels a pull back toward that place.

Cognitive · personal best (pbest)
3

Follow the most successful bird

Birds watch each other. Everyone feels a pull toward the best spot that any bird in the flock has found.

Coefficients
Live calculation

Most implementations draw r₁ and r₂ separately for each dimension, which also slightly bends the pull's direction. Here one value per term keeps the arrows easy to read.

w · v

Inertia

Keeps the particle moving in its current direction. Large w → long sweeping flights (exploration). Small w → quick braking (exploitation).

c₁r₁(pbest − x)

Cognitive · "nostalgia"

A spring pulling the particle back toward the best place it personally has ever been. Makes each particle refine its own discoveries.

c₂r₂(gbest − x)

Social · "peer pressure"

A spring pulling toward the best place found by the whole swarm. This is how one particle's discovery spreads to everyone.

r₁, r₂ ~ U(0,1)

Randomness

Fresh random numbers every step make each pull a bit stronger or weaker. Particles don't fly rigid straight lines, so the swarm keeps diversity.

x ← x + v

The move

The three arrows are added tip-to-tail; their sum is the new velocity. The particle jumps by that vector. Repeat for every particle, every iteration.

06 · Parameters

Exploration vs. exploitation: the central trade-off

Every optimizer must balance two opposite needs. Exploration means searching widely to discover promising regions. Exploitation means focusing on a good region to refine the answer. Too much exploration and the swarm never settles; too much exploitation and it settles too early in the wrong valley — premature convergence.

w(t) = wmax − (wmax − wmin) · t / T. Explore boldly at the start, then gradually brake and fine-tune. Try "Decay 0.9 → 0.4" in the playground.

χ ≈ 0.729

Constriction factor (Clerc & Kennedy, 2002). A mathematical stability analysis showed that choosing w = 0.7298 and c₁ = c₂ = 1.49618 guarantees the swarm converges without exploding. That's why the playground's default is the "Standard" preset.

Rule of thumb

Keep c₁ ≈ c₂ so personal and social knowledge are balanced, keep w < 1 so velocities shrink over time, and use a ring topology or larger swarm when the landscape has many local minima.

07 · Social Networks

Who listens to whom? Neighbourhood topologies

"gbest" doesn't have to mean the best of the whole swarm. Each particle can instead follow the best of a small neighbourhood (lbest). The shape of this communication network changes how fast good news spreads. Below, one particle (glowing) discovers a better position. Watch how the information travels.

Global / Star topology (gbest)
informed 0/12steps 0

Everyone is connected to everyone. A discovery reaches the entire swarm in one step.

Strength
Fast convergence. Great on smooth, unimodal problems.
Weakness
If the leader is in a local minimum, everyone is dragged there.
Ring topology (lbest, k = 2)
informed 0/12steps 0

Each particle only talks to its two neighbours. Good news must travel hop by hop around the ring.

Strength
Several regions are explored in parallel → robust on multimodal landscapes.
Weakness
Slower convergence; needs more iterations.

Other common topologies: Von Neumann (grid, 4 neighbours), random, and "fully-informed" PSO where every neighbour contributes. Switch the playground to "Ring (lbest)" to see the effect on a real search.

08 · The Big Picture

Where PSO fits and why it matters

Strengths

  • Gradient-free. Needs only f(x) values — works on noisy, discontinuous or black-box functions.
  • Simple. Two update equations; a working implementation is ~20 lines.
  • Few parameters. Just w, c₁, c₂ (and N) — with well-known safe defaults.
  • Memory. Every particle keeps its best find, so good solutions are never lost.
  • Parallel. Particles are evaluated independently — easy to distribute.

Limitations

  • Premature convergence on highly multimodal problems, especially with gbest topology.
  • No guarantee of reaching the global optimum (it's a heuristic).
  • Parameter sensitivity: poor w, c₁, c₂ can stall or explode the swarm.
  • High dimensions need many particles and iterations.
  • Continuous by design; discrete problems need variants such as Binary PSO.

Key takeaways

PSO is a population-based, gradient-free optimizer inspired by the social behaviour of bird flocks.
Each particle has a position (a candidate solution), a velocity, and a memory of its personal best.
New velocity = inertia + pull toward pbest + pull toward gbest, each scaled by a coefficient and a random number.
w controls exploration vs. exploitation; c₁/c₂ balance individual vs. social learning.
The topology (global vs. ring) controls how fast information spreads — speed vs. robustness.
It's a heuristic: very effective in practice, but with no guarantee of finding the global optimum.
